Inside Hitman Cricket

A 3D cricket game that runs entirely on your phone, plus a small scorers’ box on the internet that remembers who is best.

Built by describing what I wanted to Claude, one conversation at a time. This page explains what got built underneath.

01 · The big picture

Three places, one game

Think of a cricket ground. The play happens on the ground: that is your phone. The scorers sit in a box and write down what matters: those are six small helpers on Vercel. The scorebook keeps it all after everyone goes home: that is the database.

Almost everything lives on the phone. The internet is only used to share and remember things between players.

Nothing on this screen is a downloaded 3D model. The stadium, crowd, players and bat are all built out of simple shapes by code.

02 · Opening the link

From tap to cover

Vercel acts like a shop window: it hands over the game’s files. Then the phone does the rest. Before loading anything heavy, it asks three quick questions.

Why check for private windows? A private window forgets you when it closes, so a place won on the board from one could never be improved. The game can’t know for sure, so it guesses from how much storage the browser offers.

03 · One ball, start to finish

Every ball is a loop

The game is a loop of five steps, thirty times over. None of it needs the internet. The only moment that matters is when your swipe lands, measured against when the ball reaches the bat.

Drawn to scale. A perfect window is 80 ms wide, less than a blink. That is why sixes feel earned.

The bowler reads you

After three sixes he bowls a yorker. After four quick balls, a slower one. Every over of pace has a bouncer somewhere in it.

Confidence builds

Sixes add 28, fours 22. A miss takes 18, a wicket empties it. Full, it unlocks the charge down the pitch and the scoops.

Same seed, same innings

Deliveries come from a numbered “seed”, so a test can replay exactly the same thirty balls and check the result.

04 · Who you are

A player with no login

There are no accounts and no passwords. Instead, the first time you play, your phone makes up a long random ID card for you. To avoid losing it, it keeps a copy in three different pockets.

The career key is the spare house key. Three cricket words and two digits, made up by the helper when you claim a name. Write it down, and a new phone can get your whole career back. The scorebook never stores the key itself, only a scrambled fingerprint of it, so even someone reading the database can’t see it.

Your name and kit are kept apart

Saved on the phone too, but separately. Losing those costs one tap to pick again. Losing the ID would cost a place on the board.

A name belongs to one person

“Big Show”, “bigshow” and “Bíg Shów” count as the same name. Once claimed, it is never given to anyone else, even after a bad day.

The trick: a whole innings as one number

A leaderboard has tie-breaks: runs first, then sixes, then fours, then fewer wickets, then fewer dot balls, then whoever got there first. Instead of asking the database to juggle six rules, every innings is squeezed into one big number in that order, like sorting words alphabetically. Then the ladder can sort 50 people instantly.

The top half needs no internet at all. The bottom half is where the three lanes talk.

Opening the Top 50

GET /api/board

Everyone gets the same list, so the helper can remember it for ten seconds. A hundred people opening the board at once cost one trip to the scorebook, not a hundred.

Claiming a place

POST /api/score

Only when the player taps to put an innings on the board. The helper runs six checks in order, cheapest first, and only then writes.

Every innings counts toward a career

POST /api/innings

After every finished innings, good or bad, with no button to press. “Most runs ever” would be a lie if it only added up the good days.

The career card

GET /api/career

The phone shows its saved copy first, so the card appears instantly, then swaps in the fresh numbers. The card is painted as a picture, and that same picture is what gets shared.

Getting a career back with a key

POST /api/restore

Browsers forget. The career key is the spare house key a player writes down. The scorebook never keeps the key itself, only a scrambled fingerprint of it.

The feedback form

POST /api/feedback

Ten taps and one optional box. Nothing is sent until the last screen. Answers go into one list that keeps the newest 2,000 and throws the oldest away.

The career card’s tier is its material

Read off career runs in The Blast, or balls faced in Test Survival. The thresholds came from real play data: roughly 140 runs a day for a typical player.

Sharing never touches the server

The innings card and career card are painted as pictures on the phone, using a canvas. That picture goes straight to the phone’s share sheet: a PNG for WhatsApp, a tall 1080 × 1920 JPEG for Instagram stories.

The card you see is the card you send: they are the same picture, so they can never drift apart.

Counting without knowing

GoatCounter gets named moments like innings-end or score-50-74. Scores go out as ranges, never exact numbers, and never with a name.

07 · How the rigs were built

Puppets made of maths

There is no Blender file, no motion capture and no character pack. Every player is built from simple shapes, and every shot is a handful of poses placed by hand, checked against real broadcast footage.

Move the hand. The elbow works itself out.

This is the “two-bone solver”, the heart of the rig. You never place an elbow or a knee. You say where the hand or foot should be, and the maths finds the only place the joint can go with bones of fixed length. Try it: drag anywhere in the box.

Reach: –

The dashed circle is as far as the arm can reach. Past it, the arm stays straight at full length instead of stretching. A test checks this for every figure in every frame.

A pose is a short list of numbers

Like a layer’s properties panel in a design tool. Each shot has a few of these: the guard, the moment of contact, the follow-through, the finish, and sometimes a “recover” pose so the bat comes home without passing through the batter’s head.

From these few numbers, the solver places the knees and elbows, both gloves lock onto the handle, and the bat turns smoothly between poses instead of spinning the short way through the body.

Why so few?

Fewer numbers means fewer things to get wrong. A new shot is a new list, not a new animation file. Ten strokes live in one code file this way.

Real reference, not guesswork

Shots like the charge, the scoop and the cover charge were each rebuilt from two broadcast clips: one from behind the batter, one from the bowler’s end.

The charge down the pitch, frame by frame

A real “contact sheet” rendered by the game’s own rig tool. This is how each change was reviewed: look at the frames, compare with the footage, say what is off.

The bowler: same body, his own clock

The bowler and fielders share one body design with one set of bone lengths. His action always takes the same time, whatever the ball does, so the only way to read a slower ball is to watch the ball.

08 · Shipping a change

From idea to live

Every change starts on a branch, a safe copy of the code. Vercel builds a private preview of it with its own practice drawers. Only after the checks pass and the branch is merged does the real site change.

Robot testers

Scripts open a real browser, play a full innings, finish it, open the board and check the screen. They catch what code tests can’t see.

Rule tests

Hundreds of small checks: timing windows, scores, tie-breaks, the rig’s limits. They run in seconds.

The one secret

The database password lives only in Vercel’s settings, never in the code or a chat. If it is ever seen, it gets changed.

09 · Words you’ll hear

A pocket glossary

FrontendEverything that runs on the player’s phone. Here, that is nearly the whole game.
BackendCode that runs on someone else’s computer on the internet. Here, six small helpers.
Serverless functionA helper that sleeps until asked, does one job, and goes back to sleep. You pay only while it works.
API / endpointAn address a helper answers at, like /api/board. The phone “calls” it and gets an answer.
DatabaseWhere things are kept after the phone forgets them. Here, Upstash Redis.
RedisA very fast database built from simple drawers: lists, labelled pockets and self-sorting ladders.
Sorted setA Redis drawer that keeps itself in order by a number. Perfect for a leaderboard.
CacheShort-term memory. Remembering an answer for a few seconds so nobody has to fetch it again.
Hash (scrambling)A one-way blender. Easy to scramble a key; impossible to unscramble it back.
three.jsThe library that draws 3D in a web browser: the ground, the crowd, the players.
CanvasA blank picture on the page that code paints on. The share cards are painted there.
TypeScriptThe language the game is written in. JavaScript with labels that catch mistakes early.
ViteThe tool that bundles all the code and art into files a browser can load fast.
VercelThe host. It serves the game files and runs the six helpers.
Branch / mergeA safe copy of the code to try things on, then folding it back into the real one.
RigThe skeleton inside a character, and the rules for how its joints move.
Keyframe / key poseA pose placed by hand at a moment in time. The frames between are filled in.
IK (two-bone solver)Say where the hand goes; the maths works out where the elbow must be.
